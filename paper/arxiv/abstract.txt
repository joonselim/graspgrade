Robot hand teams want human egocentric video because it is cheap and plentiful, but most of it cannot teach a robot hand. Today clips are picked by looking at the video only, and there is no way to check the choice against real contact or a real robot. GraspGrade cuts human egocentric video into clips at the moment of contact and gives each clip a score from 0 to 100 for how likely it is that a robot hand can copy the grasp. Video is the only input. We build ground truth for both steps from sensors, a touch-sensing glove for contact timing and motion capture of hands and objects for robot copyability, and use it to tune and score methods built from Meta SAM 3.1 and Muse Spark. The main finding is that the sensors change which video-only method looks best. On 22 held-out glove episodes, a vision language model asked directly about touch, plus one timing shift learned from the glove on tuning episodes, gives the best contact timing of the arms we compare (boundary F1 0.195 at +/-5 frames, 0.376 at +/-15 frames, contact IoU 0.850). On 1,426 held-out motion capture clips, the same model asked the plain question "can a robot hand copy this?" is at chance (AUC 0.501), while a checklist of object and hand questions reaches 0.617 with equal weights, and our score of twelve checks reaches 0.624. The gain comes from the checklist (+0.117), not from learning weights on labels (+0.005). The object name alone reaches 0.771 when the object was labeled before, and our score keeps 0.571 on objects it has never seen. The score is for rigid objects: a step meant to hold soft objects back misses its pre-registered rule. Both ground truths are released as label files with standalone scorers. Every comparison, threshold and pass rule was written down before the held-out data was scored.
